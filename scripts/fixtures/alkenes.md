# Organic Chemistry I, Lecture 9: Reactions of alkenes and alkyl halides

## Electrophilic addition of HX
An alkene's pi bond is a nucleophile. With HBr, the pi electrons take the proton and leave a carbocation, which bromide then attacks. Propene gives 2-bromopropane, not 1-bromopropane: the proton goes to the carbon that already has more hydrogens (Markovnikov's rule), because that leaves the more stable secondary carbocation. Carbocation stability: tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl, from hyperconjugation and induction by alkyl groups.

Because a carbocation forms, rearrangements happen. 3-methylbut-1-ene with HBr gives mostly 2-bromo-2-methylbutane: the first secondary carbocation shifts a hydride to become tertiary before bromide adds.

With peroxides (ROOR) and light, HBr adds anti-Markovnikov by a radical chain: propene gives 1-bromopropane. This works for HBr only, not HCl or HI.

## Hydration and hydroboration
Acid-catalysed hydration (H2O with H2SO4) is Markovnikov and goes through a carbocation: 2-methylpropene gives tert-butanol (2-methylpropan-2-ol). Rearrangements are possible.

Hydroboration-oxidation (BH3·THF, then H2O2 and NaOH) is anti-Markovnikov and syn: the H and OH add to the same face, and there's no carbocation, so no rearrangement. 1-methylcyclohexene gives trans-2-methylcyclohexan-1-ol.

## Halogenation
Br2 adds anti through a cyclic bromonium ion. cis-but-2-ene gives racemic (2R,3R)- and (2S,3S)-2,3-dibromobutane; trans-but-2-ene gives meso-2,3-dibromobutane. In water, Br2 gives a bromohydrin, OH on the more substituted carbon.

## Substitution: SN2 and SN1
SN2 is one concerted step: the nucleophile attacks from the back side and the configuration inverts (Walden inversion). (R)-2-bromobutane with sodium hydroxide gives (S)-butan-2-ol. Rate = k[substrate][nucleophile]. Methyl > primary > secondary; tertiary doesn't react by SN2 (steric hindrance). Polar aprotic solvents (acetone, DMSO, DMF) speed it up because they don't cage the nucleophile.

SN1 goes through a carbocation, so the rate depends only on the substrate: rate = k[substrate]. Tertiary > secondary; primary doesn't. Polar protic solvents (water, ethanol) stabilise the carbocation. A stereocentre racemises because the planar carbocation can be attacked from either face. 2-bromo-2-methylpropane in water gives tert-butanol.

Leaving groups: I- > Br- > Cl- > F-; weak bases leave best. Hydroxide is a poor leaving group until protonated.

## Elimination: E2 and E1
E2 is concerted and needs the H and the leaving group anti-periplanar. Strong bases (ethoxide, tert-butoxide) favour it. With sodium ethoxide, 2-bromobutane gives mostly but-2-ene (Zaitsev: the more substituted alkene). The bulky base potassium tert-butoxide gives mostly but-1-ene (Hofmann product).

E1 goes through the same carbocation as SN1 and competes with it; heat favours elimination. Dehydration of alcohols with H2SO4 and heat is E1 for secondary and tertiary alcohols: cyclohexanol gives cyclohexene.

## Choosing a mechanism
- Methyl or primary substrate, good nucleophile: SN2 (E2 with a bulky strong base).
- Tertiary substrate, strong base: E2. Tertiary, weak nucleophile or base in a protic solvent: SN1 and E1.
- Secondary: SN2 with a good nucleophile that is a weak base (azide, cyanide, iodide) in a polar aprotic solvent; E2 with a strong base.

## Acidity
pKa: ethanol 16, water 15.7, phenol 10, acetic acid 4.8. The more stable the conjugate base, the stronger the acid: phenoxide is stabilised by resonance, acetate more so.

## Spectroscopy reminders
IR: a broad O-H stretch at 3200-3550 cm^-1 for alcohols; C=O near 1715 cm^-1 for ketones; C=C near 1650 cm^-1. 1H NMR of ethanol: three signals, a triplet (CH3), a quartet (CH2) and the OH.
